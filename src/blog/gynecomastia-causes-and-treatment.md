Gynecomastia means enlarged breast tissue in men. It is very common, completely benign in most cases, and often a source of embarrassment that men find hard to talk about. The good news is that it is well understood. In many teenagers it settles on its own, and when it does not, there are effective treatments.

In this guide, Dr. Junaid Fateh explains what causes gynecomastia, how to tell whether it is likely to settle, and what treatment and recovery involve.

## What is gynecomastia?

Men have a small amount of breast tissue behind the nipple. Gynecomastia happens when this glandular tissue grows, creating a firm or rubbery disc under the nipple and areola. It can affect one side or both, and it is sometimes tender.

It is worth knowing the difference between two conditions that look similar:

- **True gynecomastia:** growth of glandular breast tissue. You can usually feel a firm disc directly under the nipple.
- **Pseudogynecomastia:** fullness caused by fat rather than gland. The chest feels soft all over, with no distinct disc.

Many men have a mix of both. The balance between gland and fat affects which treatment works best.

## What causes it?

Gynecomastia usually comes down to a shift in the balance between oestrogen and testosterone. Common reasons include:

- **Puberty:** hormone levels swing during the teenage years, and temporary breast growth is common.
- **Excess body weight:** fat tissue converts some testosterone into oestrogen, and also adds fat to the chest.
- **Anabolic steroids and some bodybuilding supplements:** a frequent cause in young men who train at the gym.
- **Certain medicines:** including some used for prostate problems, heart conditions, stomach ulcers and mental health. Never stop a prescribed medicine without speaking to the doctor who prescribed it.
- **Ageing:** testosterone naturally falls with age.
- **Medical conditions:** such as liver or kidney disease, thyroid problems or low testosterone. Rarely, a hormone-producing tumour is responsible.
- **Alcohol and recreational drugs:** heavy alcohol use and cannabis have both been linked to it.

In many men, no specific cause is found.

## Pubertal or persistent: will it go away?

This is the question most families ask. As a general guide:

| | Pubertal gynecomastia | Persistent or adult gynecomastia |
|---|---|---|
| When it appears | During puberty, usually early to mid teens | Lasts beyond the late teens, or appears in adulthood |
| Typical course | Often settles over months to a couple of years | Less likely to go away without treatment |
| Feel | Often tender and rubbery | Tends to become firmer and less tender over time |
| Usual first step | Reassurance, review and patience | Check for an underlying cause, then discuss treatment |

Over time, breast tissue can become more fibrous. Once that happens, it is unlikely to shrink by itself, even with weight loss or medicines.

### When to see a doctor promptly

Most gynecomastia is harmless, but see a doctor without delay if you notice:

- A hard lump, especially on one side or not centred behind the nipple
- Discharge from the nipple, particularly if it is bloody
- Dimpling or changes in the skin, or a nipple that pulls inward
- Lumps in the armpit
- Rapid growth over a short time, or a lump in a testicle

These signs do not usually mean anything serious, but they need checking.

## Treatment options

### Treating the cause

If a medicine, supplement or medical condition is responsible, dealing with that comes first. Stopping steroid use and reaching a healthy weight can make a real difference, especially when the change is recent.

### Medication

For recent, tender breast growth, a physician or endocrinologist may sometimes consider medicine. It tends to be much less useful once the tissue has been present for a long time.

### Surgery

When gynecomastia has persisted and bothers you, surgery offers the most dependable correction. Options Dr. Junaid may recommend include:

- **Liposuction:** removes fatty fullness through small incisions. It suits chests that are mainly fat. See [liposuction](/procedures/liposuction/).
- **Gland excision:** removes the firm disc through a small incision, usually at the lower edge of the areola, where the scar blends in well.
- **Combined approach:** liposuction to shape the chest, plus gland removal for the firm tissue. This combination is often needed.
- **Skin reduction:** for larger chests or after major weight loss, when loose skin needs removing. This leaves longer scars, which Dr. Junaid will discuss with you.
- **Nipple and areola adjustment:** sometimes added if the areola is enlarged or puffy. See [nipple correction](/procedures/nipple-correction/).

For teenagers, surgery is usually considered only once puberty is complete and the chest has been stable for some time. Dr. Junaid talks this through with both the young person and their parents.

## What recovery involves

Gynecomastia surgery is usually a day-case procedure. A typical recovery looks like this, though everyone heals differently:

- **First few days:** soreness, swelling and bruising, managed with medicines. A compression vest is worn day and night.
- **About one week:** many people return to desk work. Light walking is encouraged from the start.
- **Four to six weeks:** the compression vest is usually worn for this period. Heavy lifting and chest workouts are usually avoided until then.
- **Three to six months:** swelling settles fully and the final shape becomes clear.

If you can choose, the cooler months make wearing a compression vest more comfortable than the heat of a Pakistani summer.

### Risks

Like any surgery, gynecomastia correction carries risks, including bleeding, a collection of fluid, infection, uneven contour, asymmetry, changes in nipple sensation and visible scarring. Dr. Junaid goes through these with you at consultation and explains how they are minimised.

## About Burn Reconstruction

A burn heals in two stages. First the wound closes, often after weeks of dressings or grafting. Then, over the following months, the new scar can thicken, tighten and pull. Burn reconstruction deals with that second stage: releasing scars that limit movement, replacing tight or fragile skin, and improving how the area looks and feels.

In Pakistan, burns often come from everyday sources, such as hot tea or water spilled on young children, gas cylinder and heater accidents in winter, kitchen flames and electrical injuries at work. Scars across the neck, armpit, elbow, hand and fingers are especially troublesome because they cross joints, and in children they can hold back normal growth.

Dr. Junaid Fateh is a plastic and reconstructive surgeon who trained at Jinnah Burn & Reconstructive Surgery Centre in Lahore, and burn reconstruction sits at the centre of his hand and reconstructive work. He treats adults and children, for scars from recent burns as well as contractures that have been present for years. If a burn has only just happened, cool it under running water for 20 minutes and go to the nearest emergency department or burns unit. Reconstruction comes later.

## How Dr. Junaid approaches burn reconstruction

The first consultation is about building a plan, not booking a single operation. Dr. Junaid looks at every scar, measures how far each joint moves, and asks what troubles you most: pain, itching, an eyelid that will not close, a hand that cannot open, or simply how the scar looks. Together you agree on priorities, and areas that affect function or safety usually come first.

Timing is important. Scars keep changing for a year or more, and operating too early can lead to further tightening. While the scar matures, he may recommend pressure garments, silicone, massage, splints or steroid injections. Surgery is brought forward only when a contracture is causing real harm.

When surgery is right, he chooses the simplest technique that will do the job well, uses skin that matches as closely as possible, and plans incisions so new scars run in kinder directions. He is honest that a burn scar cannot be erased. The aim is better movement, comfort and appearance, and results vary between people. Like any surgery, burn reconstruction carries risks, and Dr. Junaid goes through them with you at consultation.

Aftercare is shared with you and your family. His team will teach you how to use splints, stretches and garments before you go home.

## Techniques and options

Options Dr. Junaid may recommend include:

- **Contracture release with skin grafting:** Cutting across a tight band of scar so the joint can straighten, then covering the gap with a skin graft.
- **Z-plasty and local flaps:** Rearranging nearby skin in zigzag or triangular patterns to lengthen a scar band and change its direction.
- **Full-thickness skin grafts:** Thicker grafts that tighten less, well suited to the face, hands and other visible areas.
- **Regional and distant flaps:** Moving skin and tissue with its own blood supply to cover joints, tendons or areas that need durable padding.
- **Tissue expansion:** Gradually stretching healthy skin next to a scar over several weeks so it can replace the scar with matching skin.
- **Fat grafting:** Placing your own fat under a stuck-down scar to soften it and improve contour.
- **Non-surgical scar care:** Pressure garments, silicone, steroid injections and, where suitable, laser treatment to flatten and soften raised scars.

## What to expect on the day

Most burn reconstruction is done under general anaesthetic, and you may stay in hospital for one to five days depending on the size of the area. You wake with a firm dressing and, over joints, a splint. Before discharge, the team explains dressing care, splint use and your first follow-up visit.
